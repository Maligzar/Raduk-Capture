# Planetary capture ranked: where language actually decides

For the high-frame-rate planetary workload RadukCapture actually serves, the ranking is **SharpCap, then FireCapture, then ASICap, then RadukCapture** — and N.I.N.A. and Sequence Generator Pro do not belong in that ranking at all, because N.I.N.A.'s tree contains no SER or AVI writer of any kind and SGPro's own vendor FAQ states it "is not currently compatible with planetary or video cameras" ([FileTypeEnum.cs](https://github.com/isbeorn/nina/blob/develop/NINA.Core/Enum/FileTypeEnum.cs), [SGPro FAQ](https://www.sequencegeneratorpro.com/faq_category/sgpro-help/)). The language-and-runtime question that motivates much of this comparison produces a negative result that has to be carried honestly: across every practitioner thread, bug report and vendor statement gathered, **not one attributes an astronomy-capture frame drop to garbage collection**. Every documented cause is USB link bandwidth, cable or hub topology, sustained disk write throughput, software buffer depth, ROI size, or CPU saturation during save. The commonly repeated interop objection fails on arithmetic too: a P/Invoke call measured at **~46 ns** and a JNI call at **~22–65 ns** are 0.001–0.005% of the **5,000,000 ns** frame budget at 200 fps. Where language choice *does* decide outcomes is narrower and more specific than folklore assumes — cross-platform reach, the measured gulf between GDI+ at **32 fps** and Direct2D at **415 fps** in Microsoft's own test, the memory-safety posture of a C++ application running a hand-rolled network listener, and the size of the pool of people who could ever contribute. RadukCapture's honest position in that field is fourth of four: its capture architecture and its correctness discipline are genuinely good, its device breadth (one camera, one focuser, one mount family), its distribution (none) and its licence (PolyForm Noncommercial, not open source) are not, and its headline throughput numbers are still emulator-derived rather than measured against real silicon over real USB.

## A network block put four of six vendors behind a search index

This limitation applies to the whole report and should be read before any vendor-sourced fact in it. The research environment's egress policy denied direct HTTPS access to **firecapture.de, sharpcap.co.uk (including its docs and forums), zwoastro.com, sequencegeneratorpro.com, nighttime-imaging.eu, cloudynights.com and archive.org**. Almost every vendor-sourced claim below therefore arrived through a search layer that read and quoted those pages server-side, not through a page anyone rendered and read end to end. Quoted strings are second-hand transcriptions. Prices, build numbers and release dates in particular should be re-verified against the live pages before anything here is published or acted on; the **£14/year** SharpCap Pro figure is the 2024 price with no later announcement found, and the SGPro **US$149 then US$59/year** figure was never read off a live checkout page ([SharpCap Pro FAQ](https://www.sharpcap.co.uk/?p=411), [SGPro subscription page](https://www.sequencegeneratorpro.com/product/sequence-generator-pro-subscription/)).

Two exceptions carry the strongest evidence in the set. **N.I.N.A. was examined from a full clone** of `github.com/isbeorn/nina` at commit `4742afa` (2026-10-17), so its 2,316 `.cs` files / 436,507 lines, its 19 real projects, its `net10.0-windows` target frameworks, its 8,152 commits since 2016-06-08 and its absent SER/AVI writer are all directly measured and reproducible facts, not inferences. **The ZWO SDK findings** came from directly fetched GitHub sources, notably a third-party binding's platform matrix and its catalogue of header defects ([ZWOptical.SDK PR #11](https://github.com/SharpAstro/ZWOptical.SDK/pull/11)). RadukCapture's figures were measured against its own working tree on 2026-10-18.

Five things could not be established and are presented here as open questions rather than as absences, because inferring them would be inventing them. **ASIStudio's UI toolkit is unknown** — no source anywhere names Qt in connection with the product, and the C++/Qt reading is roughly a 70% inference from install size, the three-platform matrix and the native C SDK underneath. **SGPro's UI framework is unknown**; the installer prerequisite list naming both .NET Framework 3.0–3.5 and 4.7.2 is as consistent with WPF as with WinForms ([4.5.0.1652 release post](https://forum.sequencegeneratorpro.com/t/sequence-generator-pro-4-5-0-1652-is-released-for-general-use/23933)). **Whether SharpCap 4.x has moved off .NET Framework 4.8 is unresolved**: the downloads page extract says 4.1 "now runs on Microsoft .NET 8 (previously .NET 7)" and ships self-contained, while the last developer-sourced requirement statement in hand is .NET Framework 4.8 for the 3.3 line ([downloads page](https://www.sharpcap.co.uk/sharpcap/downloads), [forum](https://forums.sharpcap.co.uk/viewtopic.php?p=18348)); both are second-hand and they are not reconciled. **FireCapture's UI toolkit is unknown** — Java is certain from four independent angles, Swing is not evidenced. And **ASICap's timestamping and buffering behaviour is entirely undocumented**, which matters directly to the ranking below.

Two claims that circulate about FireCapture are explicitly *not* supported by anything gathered and do not appear in this report's reasoning: that its UI is "dated" (no supporting quote was found anywhere), and that the community harbours abandonment concerns (no such discussion was found). FireCapture's maintenance position is argued here from release-date evidence alone.

## N.I.N.A. and SGPro are excellent at a job RadukCapture does not do

The cleanest way to see that these are not competitors is structural rather than rhetorical. **N.I.N.A.'s `FileTypeEnum` enumerates exactly TIFF, FITS, XISF and RAW**, writer implementations exist only for FITS and XISF, and a repository-wide grep for `AviWriter`, `.avi` and `.ser` across 436,507 lines matched only a vendored Accord test-images file ([FileTypeEnum.cs](https://github.com/isbeorn/nina/blob/develop/NINA.Core/Enum/FileTypeEnum.cs)). `ICamera` carries no frame-rate property, no queue depth and no ring buffer; live view is `Task<IExposureData> DownloadLiveView(CancellationToken)` polled in a loop from a WPF view model ([ICamera.cs](https://github.com/isbeorn/nina/blob/develop/NINA.Equipment/Interfaces/ICamera.cs)). The maintainer's own position on the one planetary-adjacent feature N.I.N.A. has is that ROI "in general is used to increase the frame rate of the camera for planetary imaging, which Firecapture is most geared towards" and that N.I.N.A.'s implementation is "so uncompelling that I would rather see the concept removed" — a quotation from a Bitbucket issue that now returns 404 and reached this research only through a cached summary, so treat it as strongly indicative rather than verified ([bitbucket issue 381](https://bitbucket.org/Isbeorn/nina/issues/381/subframing-or-region-of-interest-support)). SGPro is more explicit still: the vendor lists "Video or planetary cameras" among the notable exceptions to its equipment support and states the product "is designed primarily for Deep Space Object capture" ([SGPro FAQ](https://www.sequencegeneratorpro.com/faq_category/sgpro-help/)). A COM-marshalled `ImageArray` per exposure is structurally incapable of sustained high-rate streaming, so the exclusion is architectural, not a policy that could be reversed with a checkbox.

That is not a criticism of either product, and the deep-sky comparison runs the other way hard. N.I.N.A. is free under **MPL-2.0**, covers all eleven standard ASCOM device classes plus ASCOM Alpaca Direct plus roughly a dozen native vendor camera SDK families (ASI, QHY, ToupTek-alike, Player One, SVBony, Atik, FLI, SBIG, Moravian, Canon EDSDK, Nikon), ships eight plate-solver back-ends, three guider integrations plus direct pulse guiding, two autofocus measurement methods with four curve fits, automated meridian flip, a framing assistant and a flat wizard, and supports **109 distinct third-party plugins** composed at runtime through MEF ([nina.plugin.manifests](https://github.com/isbeorn/nina.plugin.manifests/tree/main/manifests)). Its stable release is **3.2 (2025-11-12)** with **3.3 in nightly at 3.3.0.1068**, the latter migrating to **.NET 10** ([RELEASE_NOTES.md](https://github.com/isbeorn/nina/blob/develop/RELEASE_NOTES.md)). Its costs are visible in its own tracker: a 62 MP camera on a 37-hour unrestarted session reached **13.8 GB private memory with 9 GB working set** and a stuttering sequencer tree, and a single 122 MB frame written as XISF with ZLIB plus shuffling plus SHA-512 took **3.5–4 minutes** to finalize, which the maintainer declined to treat as a bug, recommending LZ4 or Zstandard instead ([issue #417](https://github.com/isbeorn/nina/issues/417), [issue #416](https://github.com/isbeorn/nina/issues/416)).

SGPro's position is weaker and worth stating plainly because it bears on continuity arguments generally. Its 2026 train — **4.5 on 22 March 2026 plus six maintenance builds through 4.5.0.1652 on 3 August 2026** — is overwhelmingly plate-solver plumbing and bug fixes, with one genuinely new capability in the whole year (run a user script on sequence start), and build 1642 fixed "a memory leak caused by large sequences that will ultimately crash SGPro" ([forum 23865](https://forum.sequencegeneratorpro.com/t/sequence-generator-pro-4-5-0-1642-is-released-for-general-use/23865)). It remains on **.NET Framework 4.7.2/4.8**, a Windows-only terminal branch receiving security fixes only, while N.I.N.A. moved to .NET 8 in March 2024 and .NET 10 in the current nightly. It has an HTTP API self-documenting at `localhost:59590` but **no plugin architecture at all**. On migration between the two: the honest evidence is thread volume and asymmetry — a Cloudy Nights thread titled "First time trying out N.I.N.A. instead of SGP" runs past 45 pages, with separate threads at topic IDs 716445, 750688, 832652, 1003351 and 1005170, and **no corresponding corpus of threads about moving from N.I.N.A. to SGPro exists in any of these results**. No percentage or user count can be defended from that, and none is offered.

## The real planetary field is three products, and the fourth has no measured throughput

Strip away the sequencers and the field is SharpCap, FireCapture and ASICap, with RadukCapture as a fourth entrant. The engineering convergence between them is striking: every one is a producer/consumer pipeline with a pre-allocated in-RAM frame pool draining to a disk writer, and every one fails the same way when the drain cannot keep up. SharpCap's developer describes it directly — once the buffer fills, "the capture rate falls to whatever the SSD can absorb, and the camera's frames are discarded until a memory slot frees up," with buffer occupancy exposed as a Memory readout in the status bar and a **Speed Test button that writes a test SER file to measure the drive's real sustained rate** ([forum p=36536](https://forums.sharpcap.co.uk/viewtopic.php?p=36536)). FireCapture's author describes his RAM buffer mode in the same terms: "FC pre-allocates frame buffers so the memory doesn't need to be allocated/released all the time" ([Cloudy Nights 785873](https://www.cloudynights.com/forums/topic/785873-whats-the-magic-combination-of-settings-to-get-firecapture-to-not-run-out-of-memory/)). RadukCapture does the same thing with two lock-free SPSC rings in series and deliberately opposite overflow policies — the 64-slot preview ring overwrites the oldest unread frame, the write buffer refuses and counts an overrun rather than ever dropping silently — sized dynamically from available RAM between a 512-slot floor and a 4,096-slot ceiling, about 5 GB and roughly 34 seconds of absorbed disk stall at 1280×960 RAW8 (`src/core/RingBuffer.h`, `src/core/WriteBuffer.h:83-94`).

The throughput evidence is uneven in a way that matters. **ASICap has the best field numbers in the corpus**: 185–186 fps at 2 ms exposure with zero dropped frames writing directly to disk on an ASI174MM, reported by a user who had been dropping ~80% of frames in SharpCap, and 170 fps on an ASI290MM where the same user's FireCapture topped out between 80 and 145 ([SharpCap forum t=6663](https://forums.sharpcap.co.uk/viewtopic.php?t=6663), [Cloudy Nights 741977](https://www.cloudynights.com/topic/741977-low-frame-rate-with-asi-290-mm-in-firecapture/)). These are single-user, uncontrolled anecdotes with an acknowledged confound — the SharpCap moderator diagnosed that case as a `PreviousFrameNotProcessed` pipeline condition, i.e. plausibly a bug rather than an architectural ceiling ([SharpCap forum p=49196](https://forums.sharpcap.co.uk/viewtopic.php?p=49196)). No controlled head-to-head benchmark of any two of these applications exists anywhere in the gathered evidence. **RadukCapture has no field numbers at all.** Its 1280×960 at 120.3 fps / 147.5 MB/s and its 3840×2160 at 293 MB/s with 556 frames and zero drops are emulator results, and the project says so: the real camera over real USB has never been measured, the emulator's software render cannot produce 1.5 GB/s, and ring and write-buffer overrun behaviour under genuine load is explicitly untested (`docs/ROADMAP-Next.md:997+`, `wiki/Emulator-and-Testing.md`). That gap is the single largest unknown in RadukCapture's competitive position, and it sits on the heaviest-weighted criterion in the ranking below.

On output, SharpCap writes **SER and AVI** for video plus PNG/TIFF/FITS for stills with automatic format selection at a 5-second exposure threshold; FireCapture writes **SER, AVI, FIT and TIF** with the available set gated by bit depth, so a 16-bit mode removes AVI; ASICap writes **AVI for 8-bit and SER for 16-bit**; RadukCapture writes **SER v3 only**, with no AVI and no FITS writer anywhere in its tree ([SharpCap capture docs](https://docs.sharpcap.co.uk/4.0/11_CapturingVideosandImages.htm), [WonderPlanets capture panel](http://www.wonderplanets.de/FireCapture/help/capture.html), [ZWO user guide](https://www.zwoastro.com/2021/07/13/a-user-guide-to-asistudio-software-and-the-zwo-asi183mc-camera/)). SER-only is defensible for planetary work — it is the format the chain actually consumes, and its lack of structure is exactly why a capture app writing it is I/O-bound rather than CPU-bound. The absent FITS writer is a real gap anyway, because it is the one output that would let the project touch any deep-sky or photometric workflow, and the phase table wrongly marks it shipped (`RadukCapture-Architecture.md:1501` against `RadukCapture-Help.md:2259`).

### Timestamps: parity with SharpCap, not an advantage over it

This point was checked against the source because the convenient story is wrong. SharpCap's author has acknowledged that **the SER per-frame timestamp is the frame-arrival time from the camera SDK with no exposure-midpoint correction**, while FITS output does subtract exposure time, and that "the SER code hasn't been updated to the better estimate" ([forum p=37887](https://forums.sharpcap.co.uk/viewtopic.php?p=37887)). RadukCapture does exactly the same thing. `src/core/CaptureThread.cpp:147` takes the QPC reading via `Timestamp::Now()` immediately after `GetVideoFrameEx` returns, and `src/core/SERWriter.cpp:87` maps that onto a wall clock correlated once at `Open()`, writing `m_ftBase + (frameQPC − m_qpcBaseUs) × 10` into the trailer. That is frame-arrival time, not exposure midpoint. **This is parity, not a differentiator.** What the Phase 59 fix corrected was a genuinely worse defect — before it, the trailer recorded disk-write time and could lag the photons by up to ten seconds with a full backlog, which makes a file useless for WinJUPOS derotation while looking entirely valid. Correcting a serious bug brought the product level with the market leader; it did not put it ahead. The open opportunity is small, bounded and real: subtracting half the exposure in the writer would make RadukCapture the only product in this comparison known to stamp exposure midpoints, and it is a handful of lines in code that already has the exposure value to hand.

## Language and runtime: the evidence retires three objections and supports four

This is the analytical heart, and it has to survive contact with evidence that is unflattering to the native-code position.

**The garbage collection objection does not survive.** Searching the practitioner record for GC-attributed frame loss in astronomy capture returns nothing. The documented causes, every time, are a USB3 camera on a USB 2.0 port or behind a shared hub, cables beyond the ~5 m recommendation, insufficient sustained disk throughput, a buffer set too small, an ROI set too large, the camera's own USB traffic setting, CPU saturation during save, and one pipeline scheduling condition. The steelman for managed adequacy is strong and should be stated at full strength. A capture application does not have to process each frame within the inter-frame interval; it has to drain the queue at the average rate, so a ring of 64 pre-allocated 2 MB buffers — 128 MB resident, trivially affordable — absorbs a **320 ms** stall at 200 fps and loses nothing, which is why the standard field fix for drops in every one of these products is "increase the buffer." The mitigations on both runtimes are complete rather than partial: `ArrayPool<T>`, `GC.AllocateArray(pinned: true)` on the Pinned Object Heap and unmanaged allocation make a .NET capture loop allocation-free in steady state, and a pooled `DirectByteBuffer` does the same on the JVM. The data path is native anyway — the vendor SDK DMAs into a native buffer, and debayer and disk write can both operate on it. And SharpCap's commercial standing is itself evidence: a .NET application is the de facto standard capture tool for a large fraction of the hobby, with no GC-attributed defect visible in its public forum.

Where the managed position genuinely breaks down is **the tail, not the mean**, and the distinction is the whole argument. A ring buffer converts a pause into a latency spike rather than a loss, right up until the pause exceeds the ring depth. The specific .NET hazard is the Large Object Heap: every planetary frame buffer is an LOH object by a factor of 25–50×, since the threshold is 85,000 bytes and a 1920×1200 16-bit frame is ~4.6 MB; the LOH is not compacted by default and is collected only during Gen2 ([Microsoft Learn: LOH](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/standard/garbage-collection/large-object-heap)). A *compacting blocking* LOH collection — triggered by `CompactOnce`, by a hard memory limit, or by `ConserveMemory` under high fragmentation — is a copy of hundreds of megabytes of frame buffers, and that is not a tens-of-milliseconds event ([GC config settings](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/core/runtime-config/garbage-collector)). Fragmentation accumulates over a session rather than over a frame, and buffer sizes change every time the user changes ROI or binning mid-session, so an application that is clean for the first hour can degrade across a six-hour night — a failure that is hard to reproduce and hard to attribute. The escape hatch trades one problem for the other: `SustainedLowLatency` suppresses foreground Gen2 but does not compact, so "higher fragmentation is possible" and the heap grows ([Latency Modes](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/standard/garbage-collection/latency)). Microsoft's own advice while in a low-latency mode is to "minimize the number of new allocations, in particular allocations onto the large object heap and pinned objects" — which is Microsoft documenting that a low-latency .NET application should avoid doing exactly what a naive frame-capture loop does. The irreducible point is not speed, it is **determinism**: a C++ ring buffer has a provable worst case; a managed one has a worst case that depends on the collector, the heap state, the machine and the runtime version. For a hobbyist that is acceptable risk. For occultation timing or lucky imaging at the seeing limit it is a real, rarely-exercised limitation. That is a values judgement about tolerance for rare catastrophic loss dressed as an engineering question, and it should be presented as one.

FireCapture's Java position adds its own specifics, and they are visible in the product's own UI. Users must size the Java heap by hand, with circulating rules of thumb of "around half the physical RAM" or "25% of RAM"; a 32 GB machine would not start with a 22 GB heap because `UseCompressedOops` caps references at 32 GB; and there is a **"Force aggressive memory recovery during capture"** toggle under Misc performance settings that at least one user credits with fixing erratic captures ([Cloudy Nights 785873](https://www.cloudynights.com/forums/topic/785873-whats-the-magic-combination-of-settings-to-get-firecapture-to-not-run-out-of-memory/), [Cloudy Nights 614351](https://cloudynights.com/topic/614351-firecapture-erratic-behavior-during-capture)). A pre-allocated buffer mode plus a GC escape hatch plus a user-visible heap knob is the author designing around the collector rather than the collector solving the problem for free, and since the RAM buffer draws from that same heap, maximum in-RAM capture duration is architecturally capped in a way a 64-bit native application's is not. Against that, the JVM's pause problem is essentially solved by collector choice in 2026 — ZGC reports sub-millisecond pauses independent of heap size, comfortably inside a 5 ms frame budget ([foojay](https://foojay.io/pedia/gc-algorithms-g1-zgc-and-shenandoah/), [JEP 189](https://openjdk.org/jeps/189)) — but nothing in the evidence says which collector FireCapture requests, and G1 remains the default.

**The interop objection should be retired with arithmetic.** A no-op P/Invoke measures **~46 ns** ([gRPC PInvokeTest](https://fuchsia.googlesource.com/third_party/grpc/+/cobalt/src/csharp/Grpc.Core.Tests/PInvokeTest.cs)); an empty JNI call measures **~22 ns** on Linux and **~65 ns** in a JMH benchmark on Windows, with Panama at ~39 ns even before Windows optimisation ([dkomanov](https://dkomanov.medium.com/java-native-access-performance-cf4ce0d68ddb), [panama-dev](https://mail.openjdk.org/pipermail/panama-dev/2019-May/005450.html)). At 200 fps the frame budget is 5,000,000 ns and a capture loop makes one to five SDK calls per frame. That is **about 0.005% of the budget**. Microsoft documents that there is no marshalling cost between blittable types with the same representation on both sides ([Calling Native Functions](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/previous-versions/ms235282(v=vs.140))). What actually matters at the boundary is not call cost but marshalling *semantics*: whether the frame crosses by pointer or by copy. A 2 MB per-frame copy at 200 fps is 400 MB/s of pure memcpy, and that is significant — but it is an implementation choice available to be got right or wrong in any language, not a property of the language.

**The real-time scheduling objection is half true.** MMCSS puts a thread in the "Pro Audio" band at priorities **23–26**, above the 16–22 used for normal foreground work, and `AvSetMmThreadCharacteristics` is an ordinary `avrt.dll` export that is P/Invokable from C# and reachable from Java ([Microsoft Learn: MMCSS](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/procthread/multimedia-class-scheduler-service)). No managed application is locked out of it. The genuine asymmetry is subtler: an application can set the priority of its own threads but cannot schedule the **GC threads, the finalizer thread or the JIT**, and tiered recompilation can stall a hot loop mid-session at an unpredictable moment. RadukCapture's capture thread runs at `THREAD_PRIORITY_TIME_CRITICAL` with optional CPU pinning (`src/core/CaptureThread.cpp:85,89`), which is the right posture — but the right posture is available to every product here.

Four places where language choice genuinely decides the outcome:

**Rendering.** Microsoft's own documentation states GDI+ alpha blending "is performed by the CPU, so it does not benefit from hardware acceleration," and a Microsoft benchmark measured approximately **GDI 120 fps, GDI+ 32 fps, Direct2D hardware 415 fps** — roughly 160 with anti-aliasing ([Comparing Direct2D and GDI](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/direct2d/comparing-direct2d-and-gdi), [Introducing Direct2D](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/archive/blogs/thomasolsen/introducing-the-microsoft-direct2d-api)). That figure is Windows 7-era and test-specific, so use it directionally, but a GDI+ ceiling *below* 60 fps before any application work is a real structural fact for a live planetary preview. RadukCapture's Direct2D path with a D3D11 compute-shader debayer writing a DXGI-shared texture that D2D draws directly (`src/gpu/GPUDebayer.h`) is the strongest preview architecture in this comparison, and it is the clearest single case where the language and API choice bought something measurable. WPF's position is more nuanced than "slow": it is DirectX-backed and hardware-accelerated in the common case, but the rendering **tier is a runtime property of the user's machine, not of the application**, and documented paths including `RenderTargetBitmap` are never hardware accelerated ([Rendering Tiers](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/desktop/wpf/advanced/graphics-rendering-tiers), [Taking Advantage of Hardware](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/desktop/wpf/advanced/optimizing-performance-taking-advantage-of-hardware)). A native application has the same software-fallback risk in principle but can detect it and change strategy — this is an observability and control difference as much as a speed one.

**Cross-platform reach.** This is Java's payoff and it is demonstrated, not theoretical: FireCapture ships official Windows x64, Linux and **aarch64** builds from a one-person freeware project, and a Raspberry Pi 5 install guide uses `firecapture_2.7.14_aarch64.deb` ([astroisk.nl](https://astroisk.nl/blog/2024/03/24/imaging-the-solar-system-installing-firecapture-on-raspberry-pi-5/)). The reach is uneven in practice precisely because the camera layer is native per-vendor code that does not port for free — a QHY camera on a Pi 4 "would always crash when loading QHY driver," and a Stellarmate user found "only ZWO ASI connects." N.I.N.A. is the instructive counter-case: **.NET alone does not buy portability if the UI is WPF**, and the response to that has been a community fork rather than an official port, with issue #144 asking for Linux/macOS support standing open and labelled `wontfix` ([isbeorn/nina issues](https://github.com/isbeorn/nina/issues?q=is%3Aissue+is%3Aopen+sort%3Areactions-%2B1-desc)). Pure Win32 plus Direct2D is the most extreme position available — not merely Windows-only but Win32-only, foreclosing even a future Microsoft UI stack migration, and porting would mean rewriting all 20,809 lines of `src/gui` plus the GPU and net layers. For planetary specifically that is a coherent trade, because the planetary workflow is overwhelmingly "laptop beside the telescope" while the Pi constituency is concentrated in deep-sky automation that is bandwidth-hostile to small-board hardware anyway. It is still a permanent, irreversible choice and should be described as one.

**Memory safety, where RadukCapture has a concrete exposure.** The convergent empirical figure is ~70%: Chromium's review of 912 high/critical bugs since 2015 found about 70% were memory-safety problems, use-after-free alone at 36%, and Microsoft reported the same ~70% of its security updates over twelve years ([Chromium via Slashdot](https://tech.slashdot.org/story/20/05/24/1349204/), [GrammaTech](https://www.grammatech.com/learn/memory-management-is-the-leading-cause-of-security-vulnerabilities-in-google-chrome/), [ZDNet via CERT Bournemouth](https://cert.bournemouth.ac.uk/?p=8475)). CISA, NSA, FBI and four partner agencies published joint guidance in December 2023 urging manufacturers toward memory-safe languages, with C# and Java on the list and C++ not ([Security Boulevard](https://securityboulevard.com/2023/12/cisa-to-developers-adopt-memory-safe-programming-languages)). That guidance targets commercial manufacturers shipping to enterprise and government, so a hobbyist application is not its threat model — but the engineering posture still applies, and it applies specifically. RadukCapture runs a **hand-rolled RFC 6455 WebSocket server on Winsock2 with hand-rolled SHA-1 and base64 and a masked client-frame parser, accepting mount and focuser commands with no authentication** (`src/net/WebSocketServer.{h,cpp}`, `docs/SECURITY_REVIEW_2026-07-11.md` §5). That is attacker-influenceable length fields, masking, fragmentation and continuation frames parsed in C++ on a socket — the exact shape of the bug class the 70% figure describes, and it originated as CRITICAL finding S1 ("remote overflow + wide-open hardware control") before Phase 57 hardening. Loopback-by-default with an explicit `RADUK_WS_LAN=1` opt-in is the right default and materially reduces exposure. The honest counterweight is that a managed runtime would eliminate the spatial and temporal memory-safety classes and would not have supplied the missing authentication; a C# WebSocket server with no auth is not safer in practice than a fuzzed C++ one with a token.

**The contribution pool and the plugin ecosystem.** N.I.N.A. 3.2 gave each plugin its own `AssemblyLoadContext` so that "no more dependency clashes between different plugins" occur — a managed-runtime capability whose Win32/C++ equivalents are separate processes with IPC or manual DLL version management, both substantially more work. That runtime affordance is directly connected to an ecosystem of **109 plugins** and 78 distinct commit-author addresses across 8,152 commits, even though the founder authored roughly 79% of them. FireCapture's Java plugin API, introduced in v2.5, has produced a small but genuine third-party ecosystem (autofocus, SSSMon, FlareDetect) at the cost of requiring contributors to compile against a JRE no newer than the bundled one. RadukCapture has no plugin architecture, no extension points, no public API, no CI, no `.github` directory and 52 commits from one account — and its **PolyForm Noncommercial 1.0.0 licence is the thing that most limits its continuity story**, because it is not an open-source licence under the OSD's no-discrimination criterion, which means it is GPL-incompatible, will not be packaged by Linux distributions, and is generally unavailable to contributors whose employers run legal review ([SPDX](https://spdx.org/licenses/PolyForm-Noncommercial-1.0.0.html)). What PolyForm buys is precisely targeted — it stops a camera manufacturer shipping the capture engine while permitting every actual amateur user to use, modify and redistribute it. If the goal is protection from commercial appropriation it is the right instrument. If the goal is contributions, MPL-2.0 is the licence that matches, and N.I.N.A. is the working proof inside this very category.

One claim deliberately absent: **no developer-productivity multiplier appears anywhere in this report**, because no methodologically defensible study was found and the figures that circulate in this space are folklore. The defensible version of the velocity argument is library-level, not language-level: C#/.NET ships an async model, JSON, HTTP and WebSocket stacks, collections, a test culture and a package manager in the box, and a pure-Win32/C++20 project must select, vendor or write each one — RadukCapture hand-emits its JSON in `src/net/APEXTelemetry.h` and uses no test framework at all. The counter-argument is equally defensible for a solo, single-platform, performance-first project: there is no team to onboard and no dependency churn to absorb, and what is bought instead is zero dependency risk and total determinism, which for someone who has lived through .NET Framework → .NET Core → .NET and WinForms → WPF → WinUI → MAUI is a risk position rather than a sentiment. Where pure Win32 is genuinely weakest is **testability**: logic entangled with `WndProc` resists unit testing, and RadukCapture's own numbers show it — 13 gated tests linking 5 of 17 production `.cpp` files, 3,084 test lines against 44,754 source lines, with the GUI, device, threading and network layers verified by the emulator and by reading rather than by tests (`tests/run-tests.ps1`).

## The ranking, with its criteria and weights stated first

This ranks for the **planetary / high-frame-rate use case only**. N.I.N.A. and SGPro are excluded rather than scored, for the structural reasons given above: scoring a product with no video writer, or one whose vendor states it is incompatible with planetary cameras, on a planetary scale would produce a number that looks like information and is not. Criteria and weights:

| Criterion | Weight | What it measures |
|---|---:|---|
| Sustained lossless throughput and buffering | 25% | Evidence of real sustained capture with drop accounting; buffer design and sizing; behaviour at the ceiling |
| Output fidelity and timing correctness | 20% | SER v3 correctness, per-frame timestamps, raw data preservation, honest frame and drop counts |
| In-session planetary decision support | 15% | Centring/tracking, quality metrics, focus aids, ADC, exposure assistance — what helps while the run is happening |
| Hardware breadth | 15% | Cameras, focusers, mounts; ASCOM/Alpaca/INDI reach |
| Distribution, continuity and support posture | 15% | Installer and releases, release cadence, licence, bus factor, documentation |
| Language and runtime decisions | 10% | Determinism and worst-case bounding, memory-safety posture, rendering path, cross-platform reach, contribution pool |

The **10% weight on language and runtime is deliberate and is itself a finding**. A higher weight would be unearned: the evidence does not support treating runtime choice as a first-order determinant of planetary capture outcomes, because the failures in the field are not runtime failures. The weight is not zero either, because rendering path, cross-platform reach, memory-safety posture and ecosystem reach are all real and all traceable to language choice. If someone produced a controlled benchmark showing GC-attributed frame loss on identical hardware, this weight should rise; nothing in the current record justifies it.

| Rank | Product | Throughput (25) | Output (20) | Planetary depth (15) | Hardware (15) | Distribution (15) | Language (10) | **Weighted** |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | **SharpCap** | 8 | 8 | 9 | 9 | 9 | 6 | **8.25** |
| 2 | **FireCapture** | 7 | 8 | 9 | 8 | 6 | 7 | **7.50** |
| 3 | **ASICap** | 9 | 6 | 4 | 3 | 7 | 5 | **6.05** |
| 4 | **RadukCapture** | 6 | 8 | 7 | 1 | 1 | 7 | **5.15** |

SharpCap leads because it is the only product in the field that is simultaneously broad, maintained, distributed and measured-in-the-field, with ~14 native camera families plus ASCOM plus Alpaca, Sensor Analysis, multi-star FWHM/HFD/Bahtinov focus aids, a solar-system sequence planner and lunar/solar mosaic planning in 4.1, a working free tier, and an active 4.2 beta at build 4.2.15166.0 dated 21 September 2026. Its deductions are a stable release dating to **22 September 2023** with 4.2 in beta for over three years, a dropped-frame support burden substantial enough to warrant its own standing guide thread, and a runtime generation that could not be established. FireCapture scores level on planetary depth — AutoAlign and AutoGuide-off-the-main-camera are genuinely differentiating, and ADC tuning aids are a named strength — and ahead on language, because the aarch64 and macOS builds are a real capability nothing else here has. It loses on distribution: the 2.7 line has run since 2020 with no v3, v2.7.15's release date is contested across three candidate dates spanning March 2025 to February 2026, no release or developer communication dated in 2026 could be verified from this environment, and commercial use is prohibited by licence. ASICap wins outright on raw throughput and loses everything else — ZWO hardware only, one EAF per instance, no planet centring found, no documented timestamping, no public issue tracker, and documentation that practitioners describe as extremely lacking; the suite itself is active, at V1.19 dated 2026-03-31.

RadukCapture scores fourth, and the shape of its score is more informative than its position. **Its output score of 8 is level with SharpCap and FireCapture** — correct SER v3 with a 178-byte packed header under `static_assert`, capture-time timestamps, a per-frame sharpness sidecar, frame counts patched on close, raw sensor data preserved because calibration is preview-only by design, and drop accounting exposed in telemetry. Its planetary depth of 7 is a real score earned by real work. **Its hardware and distribution scores of 1 are not harsh; they are arithmetic.** One camera model, one focuser model, one mount family, with camera and focuser swapped by header name rather than by interface so that adding a second brand requires introducing a polymorphic abstraction that does not exist; a 792-line working ASCOM implementation whose every known driver is blacklisted and which the wiki marks "Superseded"; no installer, no tags, no releases, no signed binary, output paths hardcoded to `D:/RadukCapture/`, a `cmake_minimum_required(VERSION 4.2)` floor that will refuse to configure on most current distributions, and a build that works on essentially one machine configuration. And the throughput score of 6 is generous to a design that is genuinely well reasoned but has never been measured against real hardware.

**A deep-sky ranking would order this field completely differently**, and briefly: **N.I.N.A. first** by a wide margin on feature coverage, plugin ecosystem, device breadth, active development and zero cost; **SGPro second**, still maintained with a strong unattended-observatory story, equipment profiles, recovery mode and three default activations, but a runtime generation behind, with no plugin architecture and a paid model against a free competitor; **SharpCap third**, strong at EAA and live stacking with the best polar-alignment routine in the set but not a sequencer-first product; **ASIStudio fourth** via ASIImg, ASILive and ASIDeepStack, easy and adequate but capped at 16-bit output with recurring star-registration failures; and **FireCapture and RadukCapture do not compete at all** — "FireCapture cannot track or align deep-sky objects," and RadukCapture has no FITS writer, no sequencer, no filter wheel, no rotator, no meridian flip and an autofocus routine explicitly tuned for a planetary disc.

## Where RadukCapture genuinely leads, and where it is simply behind

Four things in RadukCapture are better than what the competition does, and they should not be undersold. **The emulator is the clearest technical differentiator in the project.** `src/core/CameraEmulator.h` is 1,316 lines rendering synthetic RAW8/RAW16 frames with per-planet discs, surface rotation, moons, turbulence, defocus blur and per-pixel noise; `src/core/MountEmulator.h` implements the real `IMount` interface and exposes `GetPointingErrorArcsec()`, which the camera emulator uses to render the planet *displaced from centre*, so guide pulses shrink the error and the planet re-centres and **the full `CentroidTracker` correction loop closes in software**. Thirteen environment switches inject faults — seeing wander, 0.3 arcsec/s tracking drift, camera rotation, an unaligned mount, and `RADUK_EMU_REMOVE_AFTER_S` to simulate the camera being unplugged mid-run so the self-healing path is tested without pulling a cable. Simulator camera drivers exist for SharpCap and N.I.N.A. and ASCOM simulators are available to anyone; a *coupled* physical model where the guide loop closes against rendered pixel displacement is not a standard feature anywhere in this field.

**Guide calibration is measured rather than assumed.** `src/core/GuideSolve.h` pulses east, observes where the centroid went, pulses north, repeats, and derives a 2×2 matrix from pulse milliseconds to pixels that already contains the camera's rotation in the focuser, the mirror flip of the optical train and the sign of each axis — with a near-zero determinant detected as parallel responses and the solve refused. `RADUK_EMU_ROTATION_DEG` exists specifically to prove the tracker measures rather than assumes. This is the approach PHD2 uses and it is strictly better than orientation-assuming designs; the same solver is now shared by the guide loop and click-to-recentre so two sign conventions cannot diverge. **The ADC advisor is more physically careful than anything found in a mainstream capture application**: it implements Edlén (1966) refractivity and evaluates dispersion *between the edges of the band of the filter actually fitted* rather than a fixed 400/700 nm, with the site elevation feeding the pressure term and band edges validated so a nonsense band cannot return an authoritative-looking meaningless figure (`src/core/ADCAssistant.h:106-139`). FireCapture's ADC tuning aids are the nearest competitor and are a different thing — an alignment aid rather than a dispersion prediction. And **the AI assistant is local-only with no cloud path**: WinHTTP streaming to `http://localhost:11434/api/chat` running llama3.2:3b, with the security review's attack-surface table recording "Outbound to localhost:11434 (Ollama) only" and a clean secrets scan. Note the browser dashboard does call public internet APIs, so "fully local" is true of the application and the assistant, not of the dashboard.

The list of what is simply behind is longer and none of it is arguable. **No FITS writer.** **No AVI.** **No polar alignment** — and it is backlogged rather than deferred, because the 8SE is alt-az with no wedge. **No FWHM or Bahtinov focus aid**, which is what star focus needs and what the planetary-disc-tuned V-curve cannot substitute for. **No plugin architecture, no scripting, no headless mode, no command line.** **No multi-camera and no guide camera.** **One camera, one focuser, one mount family compiled in rather than configured** — and several tuning constants are compiled in too: ~2000 steps/mm at f/10 in the autofocus, an HCG floor of 182 that is an IMX678 property, a plate scale of 0.203″/px that belongs to one scope-camera pair, SER header defaults naming the ASI678MC and the 8SE. **No installer and no releases.** **Windows-only by construction.** **No CI**, 13 tests touching 5 of 17 `.cpp` files. **No authentication on the remote-control path.** And **PolyForm Noncommercial is the single thing that most limits the continuity story** — it forecloses distribution packaging, corporate contributors and GPL compatibility simultaneously, in a project whose bus factor is already 1.

There is also a documentation-drift problem worth naming because it affects how any reader should treat the project's own prose. The wiki's headline claims ~34,500 lines across 86 source files; the tree holds **44,754 lines across 100 files**, a 29.7% understatement. The wiki states "there is no automated test suite" when 13 gated tests exist. The emulator is documented at 984 lines and measures 1,316. The bright-star table's header claims 255 stars and contains 97. Two thread priorities in the wiki table disagree with the code. The project itself makes this point at `docs/ROADMAP-Next.md:853-860`, which is to its credit, but every prose number in the project needs verification against the tree.

### What would change the ranking, concretely

In descending order of weighted points returned per unit of work. **Measure the real camera over real USB.** Throughput carries 25% and currently rests on emulator figures the project itself disclaims; a clear-night FULL RAW8 run with honest drop accounting moves that score from 6 toward 8 or 9 and is worth **+0.5 to +0.75** on its own, with no code written. **Un-blacklist ASCOM and introduce a polymorphic camera abstraction.** Hardware breadth carries 15% at a score of 1; `src/core/ASCOMMount.h` is already 792 real lines of `ITelescopeV3` late binding with ~20 `Invoke` sites, so the mount half is built and switched off. Moving that score to 5 is worth **+0.6**, and it is the cheapest large move available. **Ship an installer and tagged releases, and remove the hardcoded `D:/` paths and the CMake 4.2 floor.** Distribution carries 15% at a score of 1; getting to 5 is another **+0.6** and requires no new subsystem. **Relicense to MPL-2.0, or dual-license.** This does not move the score directly but it is the precondition for the contribution pool, the distro packaging and the GPL compatibility that every other continuity improvement depends on, and N.I.N.A. is the proof it works in this category.

Two smaller items are worth doing for reasons other than score. **Subtract half the exposure from the SER trailer timestamp.** It is a bounded change in code that already has the exposure value, and it would make RadukCapture the only product here known to write exposure midpoints — turning today's parity with SharpCap into an actual differentiator for WinJUPOS derotation and occultation timing. **Fuzz the WebSocket frame parser and put a token on the command path.** It is the highest-risk component in a C++ application by the standard the memory-safety evidence sets, the loopback default is right but is not a substitute, and the fix is small. Finally, **a FITS single-frame writer** is worth more than an AVI writer: AVI is a legacy container the planetary chain does not want, while FITS is the one output that would let the product touch any photometric or deep-sky workflow and would stop the phase table from claiming a capability that does not exist.

## Conclusion

The most useful thing this comparison produced is a demolition rather than a ranking. The intuition that a native C++ capture application should beat a managed one on frame integrity is widely held, structurally plausible, and unsupported by the field record — nobody in this corpus has lost a planetary run to a garbage collector, and the best-measured throughput numbers in the entire set belong to a product whose implementation language nobody can confirm. What separates these products in practice is not runtime but reach: how many cameras they drive, how many people can install them, how many people could contribute to them, and whether the author can still be reached. On those axes RadukCapture is fourth of four, and the gap is not technical sophistication — it has more of that per line than most of the field — but surface area. One camera, one focuser, one mount, one machine, one author, one licence that keeps everyone else out.

The second finding is that the ranking's dimensions are not equally movable, and the immovable ones are not the ones that look hard. Measuring the real camera, un-blacklisting ASCOM, shipping an installer and changing the licence are four pieces of work that together move RadukCapture from 5.15 to roughly 7.2 — past ASICap and within touching distance of FireCapture — and not one of them requires inventing anything. What cannot be fixed by effort is the Win32 commitment, which is permanent and should be treated as a deliberate, coherent trade rather than something to feel defensive about: the planetary imager is sitting beside the telescope with a laptop, and Direct2D at 415 fps against GDI+ at 32 is the kind of margin that justifies a closed door. The one place the evidence says to spend differently is testing. The project reviews its own code more rigorously than most commercial software and tests it less than almost any of it; 13 tests over 5 of 17 translation units, with the lock-free buffers, the correction loop, the device layer and the entire GUI covered by the emulator and by reading, is a gap that a project this careful about correctness should find uncomfortable.
